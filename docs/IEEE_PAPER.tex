\begin{abstract}
Parental-control tools report \emph{how long} a child plays, not whether the play is
healthy or compulsive. We present a deployed system that screens for gaming risk from
\emph{how} a child plays. An Android app on the child's phone records ten behavioural
features, the child's typed chat and short voice segments during games. A cloud backend
scores behaviour, chat toxicity in English and Hindi, and voice emotion, and fuses the
three scores into one risk band, shown to the parent with reasons. Raw audio is deleted
after feature extraction. On held-out in-domain data the chat model reaches an area
under the precision--recall curve of $0.821$ on gaming chat and at least $0.95$ alert
precision in English, romanised Hindi and Devanagari; the four-class voice model
reaches $0.574$ accuracy on unseen speakers. We then tested the served behaviour score
against a gaming-disorder questionnaire (IGDS9-SF) in an anonymous survey of adult
gamers ($134$ responses, $103$ scored). They correlated at Spearman $\rho{=}0.317$
($95\%$ CI $[0.137,0.478]$). Features of the pattern of play tracked the questionnaire
better than features of its volume (difference $+0.199$, $95\%$ CI $[+0.019,+0.380]$);
the score's advantage over self-reported hours was suggestive, not established. Two
results went against the system. A hand-set genre weight had no support and was removed
from the fused score, and four of five parent-facing labels did not track the items
they were named after and were renamed. Training labels for behaviour are synthetic,
the survey covered adults, not adolescents, and clinical accuracy has not been measured.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
DSM-5~\cite{dsm5} and ICD-11~\cite{icd11} describe Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) in
terms of behaviour: impaired control over gaming, gaming taking priority over other
activities, and continued gaming despite harm. Parents usually notice late, and only
from the outside. Self-report questionnaires depend on honest answers from the child,
and deceiving others about the amount of play is itself one of the DSM-5 criteria.
Screen-time counters measure volume, which large telemetry studies find to be only
weakly related to well-being~\cite{johannes2021,vuorre2022}. To our knowledge, no
earlier system combines passive capture of several signals, a risk signal for
guardians, and a check of that signal against a clinical IGD instrument
(\S\ref{sec:related}).

We built and deployed such a system and evaluated it as a screening tool. Each channel
is tested on real held-out data where such data exist, and we state the limits where
they do not. In an adult survey, we tested the behaviour score against a clinical
questionnaire. The main correlation held; two components did not. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A deployed platform: two Android apps and a cloud backend. The behaviour model's
  only inputs are ten measured features. Ten proxy scores, derived from them by code
  that training and serving share, are shown to parents but never fed to the model, so
  SHAP~\cite{shap2017} explains only measured features.
\item Fusion that renormalises its weights over the channels present, isotonic
  calibration (expected calibration error, ECE, $0.031\!\rightarrow\!0.012$ for chat),
  and an alert threshold set for high precision.
\item Chat scoring for Indian users: Hindi in both scripts from
  HASOC~\cite{hasoc2019}; clean Hindi text~\cite{wikihi}, added so that Devanagari
  script alone does not raise the toxicity score; a custom keyboard with a Devanagari
  layout for games whose text the accessibility service cannot read; and an accent
  audit of the speech path on Svarah~\cite{svarah2023}.
\item An external check of the behaviour score against the Internet Gaming Disorder
  Scale--Short-Form (IGDS9-SF)~\cite{igds9sf} in $103$ scored survey responses,
  including two results against the system.
\item An evaluation protocol built to expose optimistic results: speaker-independent
  splits, one-component ablations with bootstrap confidence intervals, transformer
  baselines, and an automated check of the paper's survey numbers.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
Passive smartphone sensing of behaviour and mental state has a decade of precedent.
StudentLife inferred stress and academic outcomes from phone telemetry in a student
cohort~\cite{studentlife2014}. Shin and Dey detected problematic smartphone use from
passive usage features and validated it against a self-report scale~\cite{shin2013},
and Lee et al.\ explored smartphone overuse among college students~\cite{lee2014}. Our
behavioural channel follows this tradition, but it targets pattern features specific to
gaming and it serves a guardian, not the user. The weak link between play time and
well-being~\cite{johannes2021,vuorre2022} motivates our premise that the pattern of
play matters more than its volume. Work on IGD has modelled survey
data~\cite{huang2024,strojny2024} or reviewed family factors~\cite{ergin2025}, but none
deploys passive capture. Commercial tools for guardians, such as screen-time dashboards
and content monitors like Bark, do capture several signals. They report volume or
keyword hits, however, and not a validated measure of severity. Ghosh et al.\ document
the surveillance risks of such tools and what children think of
them~\cite{ghosh2018}. Our design responds to those concerns with consent, transparency
and a preference for informing the parent over blocking the child
(\S\ref{sec:ethics}). None checks a deployed, guardian-facing risk signal against a
clinical instrument~\cite{igds9sf}; that is the gap we begin to address.

\section{System Architecture}
\textbf{Data-collection tier (Child app).} A foreground service detects a game session
from the foreground package, using a parent override, then a curated allowlist, then
the operating system's game category. It records ten objective features per session.
Typed chat is captured by a custom keyboard, which, unlike the accessibility API, also
works in canvas-rendered games such as Roblox; an accessibility service is the
fallback. Typed text and short microphone segments are recorded only while a tracked
game is in the foreground, and password fields are skipped. Speech is transcribed on
the phone with Vosk~\cite{vosk} (Indian English). The transcript and a short WAV file
are sent over HTTPS; the server scores only segments with voice activity and deletes
the audio after feature extraction. Consent is versioned and fails closed: when the
consent version changes, the server refuses uploads until the parent consents again. If
the app is force-stopped, uninstalled (an optional Device Admin step obstructs this) or
loses a capture permission, the parent is alerted, so monitoring does not stop silently.

\textbf{Serving tier.} The backend is a Flask application on Render. It uses Postgres
(Neon) in production and SQLite in development, with one code path tested on both. It
scores each channel, fuses the scores, computes SHAP explanations and sends alerts to
every parent device. A weekly job checks the production scores for distribution drift.
The export and delete endpoints share one list of tables, so whatever can be exported
can also be deleted.

\textbf{Presentation tier (Parent app).} The parent sees the risk band with
plain-language reasons, a 14-day trend and alerts. Marking an alert accurate or a false
alarm feeds a Beta-posterior threshold tuner, whose suggestion is capped at $\pm0.05$
per run and applied only by a person. The parent can nudge the child, and a ``what we
can and cannot see'' screen lists the system's blind spots.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Behavioural features and labels}
The behaviour model uses ten \emph{objective} features. Five describe volume: daily
hours, weekly hours, sessions per day, session duration and days played per week. Five
describe pattern: late-night ratio, longest streak, binges per week, break between
sessions and rapid re-login ratio. Ten proxy scores with names such as ``craving'' and
``tolerance'' are computed from these ten features by one function that training and
serving share. The proxies are shown to parents and are never model inputs. We found no
public dataset that pairs per-session device telemetry with IGD labels. We rejected two
Kaggle candidates after inspection: one is synthetic and labels engagement, and the
other has no gaming sessions. The training data are therefore \emph{synthetic}: three
simulated classes with hand-set feature distributions, with play time following a
survey of $13{,}464$ gamers~\cite{sauter2017}. The base rate of severe cases ($6.4\%$
scoring $\geq$$36$), which motivates precision-first alerting, is from the open
$11{,}191$-row release of a seven-country IGDS9-SF study of League of Legends
players~\cite{igdslatam2024}. The model's accuracy therefore measures how separable the
synthetic classes are, not clinical validity. The score meets real questionnaire
answers in \S\ref{sec:survey}.

\subsection{Models}
\textbf{Behaviour.} We use a random forest (200 trees, depth 6) on the ten features,
kept after comparing tabular models because it supports exact SHAP and calibrated with
isotonic regression on a held-out split. \textbf{Chat.} TF-IDF word 1--2-grams and
within-word character 3--5-grams feed a logistic regression with isotonic calibration.
The model is trained on five real corpora: Jigsaw~\cite{jigsaw2018}, CONDA in-game
chat~\cite{conda2021}, Davidson~\cite{davidson2017}, HASOC 2019
Hindi~\cite{hasoc2019} in Devanagari and machine-romanised, and clean Hindi text from
Wikipedia~\cite{wikihi}. Its score is combined by noisy-OR with a curated lexicon of
English, Hinglish and Devanagari terms, screened against Bohra et
al.~\cite{bohra2018}. \textbf{Voice.} A histogram gradient-boosting classifier on 36
acoustic features is trained on four emotional-speech corpora
(RAVDESS~\cite{ravdess2018}, CREMA-D~\cite{cremad2014}, EMO-DB~\cite{emodb2005},
URDU~\cite{urdu2018}; $9{,}817$ clips, 154 speakers, three languages). Training uses
the serving feature extractor. Transcribed speech is also scored by the chat model.

\subsection{Fusion and alerting}
The session risk is a weighted mean of the channel scores, with hand-set weights
$0.4/0.3/0.3$ for behaviour, chat and voice, \emph{renormalised over the channels
present} so that a missing channel adds nothing. Cut-offs at $0.33$ and $0.67$ give the
low, some and high concern bands. The behaviour score is
$P(\text{high})+0.5\,P(\text{some})$ from the forest. The chat score is the maximum
over the session's typed messages. The voice score averages, over the session's
segments, a hand-set risk per emotion (angry $0.9$, frustrated $0.7$, excited $0.4$,
neutral $0.1$) weighted by intensity. A chat alert is raised at $0.95$, above the
best-F1 threshold ($0.85$), to limit false alarms: on a general stream with $3.5\%$
toxic messages, precision is $0.235$ at a threshold of $0.5$ and $0.433$ at $0.95$.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
Every model is tested on held-out data, with recorded random seeds. The voice test set
is speaker-independent: its speakers do not appear in training. The main chat result is
in-domain: it uses the CONDA validation split ($7{,}721$ lines, $21.5\%$ toxic;
Fig.~\ref{fig:pr}). Each ablation removes one component, keeps the test set fixed and
reports a bootstrap $95\%$ confidence interval (CI) from $1{,}000$ resamples. We call
one configuration better than another only when their intervals do not overlap. Because
the classes are imbalanced, we report the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) and
the area under the precision--recall curve (PR-AUC) alongside F1. Calibration is shown
directly, with top-label ECE and reliability diagrams, and not only through the Brier
score.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Per-channel results}
\textbf{Behaviour.} On the synthetic labels, test accuracy is $0.916$ and macro-F1 is
$0.918$; 5-fold cross-validation gives $0.921\pm0.002$ ($23{,}000$ training and
$5{,}750$ test rows). All errors fall between adjacent bands. Isotonic calibration
lowers the Brier score $0.134\!\rightarrow\!0.128$ and top-label ECE
$0.062\!\rightarrow\!0.015$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:rel}). In the ablation
(Table~\ref{tab:abl}), a model given only screen-time hours reaches $0.702$ and makes
errors two bands apart, which no richer feature set makes. The five pattern features
alone reach $0.902$, against $0.871$ for the five volume features.

\textbf{Chat.} The served model reaches an in-domain PR-AUC of $0.821$; the same recipe
retrained under the ablation protocol reaches $0.825$ $[0.807,0.841]$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:pr}). At the alert threshold the served model has precision $0.956$,
recall $0.428$ and MCC $0.589$. Off-the-shelf toxic-BERT~\cite{detoxify2020} has PR-AUC
$0.709$ on that split. On the HASOC Hindi test set (933 rows, $52.9\%$ offensive), at
the same threshold, the served model reaches precision $0.968$ and recall $0.425$ in
Devanagari, and $0.958$ and $0.366$ when machine-romanised. Before the Hindi corpora
were added, its Hindi recall was near zero. Under the ablation protocol, removing the
CONDA domain data lowers PR-AUC to $0.511$, removing character n-grams costs $0.030$
(intervals overlap), and removing keyword fusion lowers recall at the alert threshold
$0.363\!\rightarrow\!0.278$ (MCC $0.541\!\rightarrow\!0.472$).

\textbf{Voice.} On unseen speakers, four-class accuracy (angry, excited, frustrated,
neutral) is $0.574$ and macro-F1 is $0.568$. An earlier random split gave $0.657$; the
8.3-point gap came from speakers present in both training and test. Voice has weight
$0.3$ in fusion, and a session without voice is scored on the other channels. On one
matched speaker-independent split without augmentation, frozen embeddings of an
emotion-tuned wav2vec~2.0 model~\cite{wagner2023} reach $0.776$ where our 36 features
reach $0.553$. A distilled on-phone encoder is the next step; we do not claim it here.

\textbf{Tuning.} A grid search over 188 settings of the hand-set hyperparameters, on
training data only, led to no change. Each test set was scored once, deployed setting
against search winner, with a paired bootstrap. For voice the winner gains $+0.0021$
$[-0.0117, +0.0154]$. For chat the winner ($C{=}8$) was picked on a pool with only 7\%
gaming chat; its PR-AUC is lower ($-0.0100$ $[-0.0167, -0.0035]$), and selection on
gaming chat alone picks the deployed $C{=}1$. For behaviour the winner adds $+0.0113$
accuracy on the synthetic labels but is no better on the survey ($\rho{=}0.295$ against
$0.317$, difference $-0.022$ $[-0.073, +0.027]$).

\subsection{External construct validation}\label{sec:survey}
All behaviour results above are measured against synthetic labels. The central claim,
that \emph{how} a person plays carries a screening signal that \emph{how long} does
not, had therefore never been tested against a real instrument. We ran an anonymous web
survey of adults that paired the nine IGDS9-SF items~\cite{igds9sf} with seven
multiple-choice questions about gaming patterns. Each answer band was mapped to its
documented midpoint to give the ten objective features. Each respondent was then scored
as the deployed serving path scores a session: the fitted scaler, the calibrated forest
and the same risk formula. The main wave (7--9 Aug.\ 2026) gave $112$ raw responses.
The form stayed open, and $22$ more arrived on 13--20 Aug.\ without further recruiting.
Following a rule fixed before any late data existed, all analyses use the final $134$
raw responses. Of these, $104$ were usable ($20$ failed eligibility, $10$ an attention
check), and the $103$ who answered every pattern item were scored. Before the late
batch the correlation was $\rho{=}0.352$ $[0.158,0.521]$; the late batch lowered it,
and we report the lower value. The respondents were young ($79.6\%$ aged 18--24) and
non-clinical: IGDS9-SF totals averaged $17.6$ (SD $6.0$, range $9$--$37$), and one
respondent was in the disordered range. The model was fitted before the survey existed,
and nothing was tuned on the survey.

\textbf{Results} (Table~\ref{tab:survey}, Fig.~\ref{fig:feat}). The correlation between
the score and the IGDS9-SF total was designated the primary endpoint in the analysis
script's documentation before data collection. All other results are secondary and
exploratory, and they are not corrected for multiple comparisons. The behaviour score
correlates with the instrument at $\rho{=}0.317$; its CI excludes zero. Self-reported
weekly hours, the quantity that commercial tools report, reach $0.147$ with a CI that
includes zero. In a paired bootstrap the score is ahead of hours in $97.4\%$ of
resamples ($\Delta\rho{=}{+}0.167$, one-sided $p{\approx}0.026$), but the two-sided
interval $[-0.002,+0.341]$ just includes zero; before the late batch it excluded zero.
The advantage over the baseline is therefore suggestive, not established. The partial
correlation shows that the score is not redundant with hours: with hours partialled out
it is $0.303$, with a CI that excludes zero. Because hours were measured with a single
noisy item, this under-controls for true screen time~\cite{westfall2016} and does not
by itself show that the score is better. The premise that pattern matters more than
volume is supported by a paired test, itself a secondary analysis: the pattern
composite exceeds the volume composite by $+0.199$ ($95\%$ CI $[+0.019,+0.380]$).
Descriptively, each of the five pattern features correlates more strongly than each of
the five volume features (composites $0.330$ and $0.128$). Self-reported involvement in
toxic chat correlates with severity at $0.323$. This supports the chat premise, as does
the $r{=}{+}0.156$ we computed from the open data of~\cite{igdslatam2024}.

\textbf{Two results against the system.} Score version~1 multiplied the fused score by
a hand-set weight for the game's genre ($0.70$--$1.25$). The survey does not support
this weight. A Kruskal--Wallis test across seven genres gives $H{=}4.59$, $p{=}0.598$.
The $p$-value has risen each time the sample grew (it was $0.159$ at $n{=}80$), and the
order of the genre groups no longer matches the order the weight assumed. The test has
only $32\%$ power ($90\%$ needs $n\!\approx\!400$), so the result is weak evidence and
not a disproof. The weight was also outside the computation that the survey validated,
because the survey scored the behaviour channel alone, with no genre factor. We
therefore removed it from the served score. Removal changes the band of $34\%$ of the
$131$ sessions in a family pilot, so it shipped as a versioned change (score
version~2). Every prediction records its version, and legacy rows count as version~1.
The analysis and drift tools take the version into account, and parents of children
with version-1 history receive a one-time note in the app. The second concerns the
proxy scores shown to parents. Of the five with a matching IGDS9-SF item, four do not
track it: only ``craving'' holds ($\rho{=}0.322$), and the others range from $+0.020$
to $+0.092$. We renamed those four so that they describe behaviour.

\textbf{Not established.} We cannot report sensitivity or specificity at the
$\geq$$36$ cut-off. Only one respondent was above it, and the analysis script refuses
to compute such caseness metrics with fewer than ten positives, by a guard written
before the data arrived. At the $6.4\%$ base rate, $\approx$$157$ usable responses
would be needed. Transfer to adolescents and any longitudinal claim are also untested.
We refitted the band cut-offs to the survey ($T_1{=}0.51,T_2{=}0.83$; weighted
$\kappa{=}0.177$ with IGDS9-SF bands) and did not adopt them. The fitted $T_2$ moved by
$0.12$ when the late batch arrived, so it likely reflects the sample's lack of severe
cases, not a clinical boundary.

\textbf{What the survey validates, and common-method variance.} The survey validates
the served risk \emph{formula} applied to self-reported answers mapped to band
midpoints. It does not yet validate the passive pipeline end to end, whose inputs are
measured telemetry and not recalled bands. The predictor questions and the IGDS9-SF
items were answered by the same person in one sitting, so part of the association may
be common-method variance~\cite{podsakoff2003}. Three observations limit this concern
without removing it. First, the comparison with self-reported hours is made within one
method: hours were reported on the same form and share any inflation. However, the
score combines seven items and the baseline is one item, so part of the advantage may
come from higher reliability and not from wider coverage of the
construct~\cite{westfall2016}. Second, the pattern--volume gap is a contrast between
features, which a method effect shared by all items would not produce. Third, the
predictor questions ask how often behaviours occur, not about attitudes, which usually
weakens method effects. The clean test is still a cohort with measured telemetry
(\S\ref{sec:ethics}). The $\rho{=}0.317$ is modest: $\approx$$10\%$ shared variance,
with a wide interval at $n{=}103$; it is a medium effect by Cohen's
convention~\cite{cohen1988}. We report it as evidence that the score carries the
intended construct, not of clinical accuracy.

\subsection{Accent-fairness audit of the spoken-chat path}
If the speech recogniser mishears a harmless word as a slur, the false alert would fall
most often on the speakers it recognises worst. To test this, we transcribed all
$6{,}656$ clips of Svarah~\cite{svarah2023} (9.6~h, 117 speakers, 65 districts) with
the deployed recogniser and scored every transcript with the served chat scorer at
$0.95$. There were zero false toxicity alerts in every accent group (rule-of-three
bound $\leq0.045\%$ overall), although the overall word error rate (WER) was $38.6\%$.
On this corpus, recognition errors caused missed speech, not false alerts. Coverage is
not uniform across accents, however: WER is $34.6\%$ for speakers of Dravidian first
languages, $38.1\%$ for Indo-Aryan and $60.8\%$ for Tibeto-Burman (Bodo only, 402
clips); Tamil is lowest at $30.9\%$. It is balanced by gender ($38.4\%$ and $38.7\%$).
The fairness risk is therefore lower recall for Northeast-Indian accents, the most
valuable target for improving the speech path.

\subsection{System testing and on-device cost}
Continuous integration runs 300 automated tests: 190 backend tests, on SQLite and
Postgres~16, and 110 Android JVM tests. Seven further research-integrity checks guard
the survey analysis; one of them fails the build if the paper's headline survey numbers
differ from the stored results. A concurrency test of 288 mixed requests over 24
threads produced no errors (median response 66~ms). The API was fuzzed with
property-based tests, its dependencies were audited for known vulnerabilities, and a
MobSF~\cite{mobsf} static audit of both signed APKs found no exploitable high-severity
issue. On a Galaxy M52, in one 15-minute Roblox session per mode, sampled every minute,
the default monitoring path used $\approx$$14\%$ of one core (the game used $215\%$)
and $288$/$301$~MB mean/peak memory, with no thermal throttling. The optional mode that
runs Hindi and English recognisers together used $51$--$72\%$ and $399$/$419$~MB, above
our limits (twice the default CPU; 400~MB), so it is off by default. A consented family
pilot ran for 23 days in production and raised 60 alerts of all eight types in its
first 12 days. A later change of consent version stopped ingestion pending re-consent,
as designed.

\section{Ethics and Limitations}\label{sec:ethics}
\textbf{Ethics statement.} The family pilot was an engineering pilot, with consent,
within the authors' own extended family. It did not seek institutional review, and no
clinical conclusions are drawn from it. The IGDS9-SF survey was anonymous, voluntary
and open only to adults ($18+$). It collected no names, e-mail addresses or
identifiers, and it displayed a consent statement before the first item. The
supervising faculty member was notified in writing, by official institutional e-mail on
14~Aug.~2026, copied to the other authors. The e-mail disclosed the design and
requested direction should additional approval be required. It was sent while the form
was open: after the main wave, before close-out and before analysis of the final
sample. That is documented faculty notification. It is not an ethics-committee
determination, and we do not describe it as one. Any cohort involving minors will
require institutional review, guardian consent and age-appropriate assent. Row-level
responses are not released, because consent covered research use and not
redistribution; aggregate outputs are released.

The system is a screening tool, not a diagnostic one, and the parent dashboard says
so. It is designed for minors: a guardian's duty of care is what makes it possible and
proportionate to screen a person who has not asked to be screened. For adults the same
design would be either a self-monitoring tool or spyware, so we do not offer it.
Consent fails closed, export and deletion cover the same data, and raw audio is not
retained. Three tensions remain. \emph{Sensing blind spots.} Games are detected from
the foreground package, so games played inside a browser or another app's webview are
invisible. Closing that gap would require screen capture or URL surveillance, which we
rejected; the blind spot is disclosed to parents instead. \emph{The crisis asymmetry.}
Profanity in chat alerts a parent, but a disclosure of self-harm to the child app's
chatbot does not; the child is shown helplines instead. We chose not to hard-code a
contested clinical judgement; a tiered ``wellbeing check'' awaits clinical review.
\emph{Evidence limits.} The behaviour model's training labels are synthetic, so its
$0.916$ accuracy measures separability, and the survey does not change that. What the
survey adds is evidence that the score is related to the intended construct. Screening
accuracy is unmeasured, the respondents were adults, the design was cross-sectional,
and the voice model was trained on acted adult speech. A cohort of children that links
guardian-reported IGDS9-SF scores to measured telemetry is the main outstanding study,
and it requires ethics approval first.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
We built and deployed a multimodal screening system for gaming risk. It goes beyond
screen time: it measures behaviour, chat toxicity in English and Hindi, and voice
emotion, and fuses them into one explained risk band for the parent. Model choices were
tested on held-out data and by ablation. In a survey of adults, a secondary test
supported the design premise that the pattern of play matters more than its volume; two
components were not supported, and we report both. The system is not a validated
clinical instrument. It is a complete, deployed screening pipeline whose reported
results each trace to a runnable script. What is still missing is data from the target
population, collected under ethics approval.
